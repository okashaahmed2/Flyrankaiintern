00

Abstract

Content teams need to know which pages are losing search visibility before the drop becomes obvious — and which of those pages are worth an editor's time. This paper frames that as a binary classification problem: using only pre-decision search metrics (impressions, click-through rate, average position) over a 30,000-page anonymized slice of production search data, a Random Forest classifier is trained and compared against a transparent, hand-written priority rule on an identical held-out split. The model improves recall over the baseline (0.653 vs. 0.581) while holding precision roughly steady, lifting F1 from 0.604 to 0.638. A deliberate leakage-trap experiment — injecting an outcome-derived column into an otherwise honest feature set — pushed F1 to a fake 0.9998, confirming why that column must stay out. The result is a ranked, explainable priority queue for editorial review: a decision-support tool, not a prediction of Google's algorithm.

01

Introduction

Search rankings drift constantly — a page that performed well six months ago can quietly lose impressions, clicks, and position without anyone noticing until traffic has already fallen off a cliff. Manually reviewing every page on a site for signs of decline doesn't scale past a handful of clients. This work — under the lane Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring — asks a narrower, answerable question: given only the metrics a team already has on hand, can a model flag which pages are declining, and rank them so an editor knows where to start?

The decision this supports is concrete: a content editor opens a short, ranked list every review cycle and decides whether to refresh, expand, or retire each page. A false positive wastes editor time; a false negative lets real decline go unaddressed — both have a cost, and the design below is built around weighing that trade-off honestly rather than optimizing a single number.

02

Data

Release. The anonymized starter slice of the FlyRank internship dataset — content_refresh_anonymized.csv, 30,000 rows.

Unit of analysis. One row = one unique page (content_id), observed at a single mid-panel snapshot (March 2026).

Label. target = 1 if the page's trend_direction was "down", else 0. Class split: 54.21% declining, 45.79% stable-or-up — confirmed identical across the train and test splits (54.21% vs. 54.20%), ruling out an accidental imbalance introduced by the split itself.

Excluded, and why. Every outcome-derived column (trend_direction, trend_pct) and any data after the March snapshot — these are only knowable after the fact and would leak the answer into the features. No client names, domains, URLs, or page titles appear anywhere in this slice or in this paper.

03

Methodology

Assumption

Ninety days of a page's own search behavior — impressions, click-through rate, average position — carries enough signal to flag decline before it is obvious from a manual review.

Baseline: the honest rule

Before training anything, a transparent hand-written score sets the floor to beat:

score = impressions_90d × (1 − ctr) × (21 − avg_position.clip(1, 20))

Pages above the median score are flagged. No learning, no black box — a rule any editor can read and argue with.

Model

A RandomForestClassifier (100 trees, random_state=42) trained on three core features — impressions, CTR, and average position — over a stratified 80/20 train/test split, evaluated on the same held-out rows as the baseline.

Leakage check

To pressure-test the feature set, an outcome-derived column (trend_pct) was deliberately added to an otherwise honest feature set. F1 jumped from 0.6792 to 0.9998 — not a win, a warning sign — confirming why the final model never touches anything derived from the label.

Validation design

Evaluation was repeated with a client-grouped holdout — no client's pages appearing in both train and test — alongside the naive random split, to check whether the naive split was overstating performance through client-level leakage. (Grouped-holdout metrics are logged in the validation-audit notebook, referenced in Reproducibility below.)

05

Limitations

No causal proof

Flags correlation with decline, not the cause — a core update, a competitor move, and seasonality all look similar from these metrics alone.

Not predicting Google

Measures observable, already-happened site metrics. It never reverse-engineers the ranking algorithm.

Missing signals

Page speed, backlink loss, and technical SEO errors are not present in this data slice at all.

Single snapshot

Trained on one mid-panel window on the small anonymized sample — time-aware, multi-month validation is the natural next step.

Uneven history

Newer or low-traffic pages have thinner 90-day history, adding noise to those edge cases.

Language, deliberately

Every claim here is observed, measured, directional, decision-support — never a guarantee.

Ranked recommendations

Rather than a flat list, pages are ranked by an impact-weighted priority score: predicted decline probability × the traffic actually at stake (impressions_90d). A high-traffic page that is likely declining outranks a low-traffic edge case, even where both cross the flagging threshold.

The playbook

  1. Review the top 15–20 pages by priority score each cycle — matched to realistic editorial bandwidth, not the full flagged list.
  2. Attach a reason code from feature importance to each page: position slip, CTR problem, or both.
  3. Re-run monthly and track whether refreshed pages actually recover — the natural next validation loop.
